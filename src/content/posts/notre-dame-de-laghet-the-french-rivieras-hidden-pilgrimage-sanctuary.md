Just twenty minutes from the crowds of Nice, tucked into a green fold of the hills above La Trinité, sits one of the Côte d'Azur's most quietly moving places. The Sanctuaire de Notre-Dame de Laghet isn't on most tourist itineraries, and that's precisely why you should go. This is where locals have come for nearly four centuries — not for the view (though it's lovely), but for something harder to put into words.

## A Miracle in the Hills: The History of Laghet

The name Laghet comes from the Niçard word for "little lake" or pond, and a chapel dedicated to the Virgin Mary has stood in this valley for centuries — some accounts trace a place of worship here back to the medieval period, built by monks after an 11th-century land donation. By the early 1600s, though, the little chapel had fallen into ruin.

Everything changed in 1652. That year, pilgrims began reporting extraordinary events at Laghet: healings, unexplained recoveries, prayers answered. Word spread fast through a region that was then culturally and politically closer to Italy than to France. On 24 June 1652, the statue of the Virgin was carried in procession to Laghet by the White Penitents of Èze — a pilgrimage the parish of Èze still renews every year.

![The Baroque nave and high altar of Notre-Dame de Laghet](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Notre-Dame-de-Laghet%20%28nave%29.jpg?width=1200)
*Inside the sanctuary: the Baroque nave leading to the high altar and the venerated statue of the Virgin.*

The response was overwhelming. By November 1653, chroniclers counted tens of thousands of pilgrims and dozens of processions. In December of that year, a commission of theologians — assisted by a lawyer and a doctor, an unusually rigorous approach for the time — examined the reported miracles and concluded they were authentic. The Bishop of Nice authorised and encouraged the cult, and in 1654 laid the first stone of a new sanctuary. Carmelite friars took charge of the growing pilgrimage site.

What you see today is essentially that 17th-century sanctuary: a compact masterpiece of Italian Baroque, its ochre façade dotted with arched windows and sky-blue shutters, crowned by a domed bell tower and a gold-leafed statue of the Virgin. It has been listed as a *monument historique* since 1931, and it remains the most important Marian pilgrimage site in the Nice diocese, still drawing well over 100,000 visitors a year from across France, Italy and beyond.

## Why You Should Visit the Sanctuary

Plenty of Riviera churches are beautiful. What makes Laghet unforgettable is the **ex-votos** — and there's nothing quite like them anywhere else on the coast.

The cloister-like arcade wrapping around the central chapel is covered, floor to ceiling, with more than a thousand small painted panels. Each one was commissioned by an ordinary person to give thanks for a grace received: a survived shipwreck, a recovery from illness, an escape from a carriage or, in the more recent ones, a car, train or plane accident. Painted in a charmingly naïve, almost cartoonish folk style, they show the Virgin appearing in a corner of the sky above a sickbed or a capsizing boat, usually with the initials **VFGA** (*votum fecit, gratiam accepit* — "made a vow, obtained a grace").

![The gilded statue of Saint Joseph inside the sanctuary](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Statue-Saint-Joseph%20Notre-Dame%20de%20Laghet.jpg?width=1000)
*One of the sanctuary's devotional statues. The interiors are richly decorated in Baroque style.*

Take your time with them. Read a few names and dates and you're suddenly looking at three centuries of everyday Riviera life — its fears, its faith, its shipwrecks and its farmyards. It's part art gallery, part social history, part prayer. Even for entirely secular visitors, it's genuinely stirring.

Beyond the ex-votos, Laghet rewards you with:

- **A gorgeous Baroque interior** — the chapel, statue of the Virgin and high altar are surrounded by a beautifully restored cloister flooded with light.
- **Real peace and quiet** — at 350 m altitude in a verdant valley, it's a world away from the Promenade des Anglais, yet a short drive from it.
- **Free admission** — there's no entrance fee, and it's welcoming to visitors of every background.
- **A simple, authentic lunch** — the sanctuary runs a *table d'hôtes* serving traditional local cooking (booking required).

It's the perfect half-day escape when the beaches are packed, the weather turns, or you simply want to see the Riviera that locals keep to themselves.

## Visiting Hours & Practical Information

The sanctuary grounds are open long hours, seven days a week:

- **Monday:** 7:00 am – 9:00 pm
- **Tuesday to Saturday:** 7:00 am – 9:30 pm
- **Sunday:** 7:00 am – 8:30 pm

**Admission is free.** Guided visits of the chapel and ex-votos are typically offered free of charge, and the site includes a shop. As this is an active place of worship and pilgrimage, dress and behave respectfully, especially during Mass and services.

**Address:** Sanctuaire de Laghet, 06340 La Trinité
**Phone:** +33 4 92 41 50 50 (also for restaurant and accommodation reservations)

*Hours can change for religious feast days and the winter season — it's worth a quick call ahead if you're travelling specifically to see the interior.*

## How to Get to Notre-Dame de Laghet

Laghet sits up in the hills between Nice, La Turbie and Monaco. It's easy to reach with a car and doable — but a little more effort — without one.

### By car (easiest)

From Nice, it's roughly a **20–25 minute drive**. Take the **A8 motorway** and exit at **La Turbie / Nice-Est**, then follow the **D2204** and the small **D53** road signed to Laghet, which winds down into the valley. There is **free parking** on site. If you're coming from Monaco or Menton, the sanctuary is a similarly short hop inland via La Turbie. A GPS set to "Sanctuaire de Laghet, La Trinité" will get you there without trouble.

### By bus

Public transport goes to the town of **La Trinité** in the valley below (on the **Lignes d'Azur** network from Nice), but the sanctuary itself is several kilometres uphill from the town centre. Unless you're prepared for a steep walk, plan on a **short taxi or rideshare** for the final leg from La Trinité. During major pilgrimage dates, occasional shuttle services run — ask at the Nice tourist office or check the sanctuary's site.

### By train

Take a regional **SNCF TER** train from **Nice-Ville** toward Tende/Breil and get off at the small **Drap–Cantaron** or **La Trinité-Victor** halt. From there the sanctuary is still an uphill climb, so a **taxi for the last stretch** is the practical option. For most visitors without a car, the simplest combination is: train or bus to the valley, then a short taxi up to Laghet.

## Making the Most of Your Visit

A few local tips to round out the trip:

- **Combine it with La Turbie** and its Roman *Trophée des Alpes* monument for a rewarding half-day in the hills, both with far-reaching coastal views.
- **Go early or late** to have the cloister and ex-votos almost to yourself — and to catch the soft light on the ochre façade.
- **Bring a little time, not just a camera.** Laghet is a place that repays slowing down; sit in the courtyard for a while before you leave.

Notre-Dame de Laghet is proof that some of the French Riviera's most memorable experiences aren't on the seafront at all. Give it an afternoon and you'll come away with a very different, and quieter, picture of the Côte d'Azur — one the locals have treasured for nearly 400 years.
